The stronger signals are **not single counts**, but ratios:

`foodbanks / supermarkets`\
`pawnbrokers / banks`\
`betting shops / all retail premises`\
`debt charities / total charities`\
`takeaways / full-service grocers`\
`warm spaces / population over 65 or fuel-poor households`\
`credit unions / high-cost credit firms`\
`CQC poor-rated care / all care locations`

Also compare every ward to a **density-matched baseline**. Otherwise dense town centres look “poor” simply because all shops and charities concentrate there. For each ward, compare it with wards of similar population density, retail density and urban/rural class, then ask: *does this ward have more pawn, betting, food aid, warm-space and debt-support infrastructure than expected?*

For England, I’d build a first “revealed hardship ecology” layer from:

1. Give Food food banks
2. Open Innovations warm spaces
3. Baby Bank Alliance / Little Village baby banks
4. Charity Commission poverty/debt/housing/food/refugee/domestic-abuse charities
5. Gambling Commission premises
6. CQC care/addiction/mental-health/social-care locations
7. FSA takeaways/convenience stores/low-rated premises
8. VOA rating list or OS POI for pawnshops, launderettes, storage, cheap retail
9. FCA register for credit unions and consumer-credit firms
10. Companies House for supplementary company counts, not shop locations

That would give you a ward-level map of **how poverty is commercially exploited, publicly managed, and locally patched over**. The interesting places will be the mismatches: wards with high deprivation but little support infrastructure; wards with lots of extractive infrastructure but weak civic repair; wards where charities are doing the work that public services used to do.

### Best feature set to build first

For England wards, I’d start with these because they are real, accessible and joinable:

`foodbanks_count` from Give Food\
`gambling_premises_count` from Gambling Commission\
`takeaway_count`, `convenience_food_count`, `low_fhrs_count` from FHRS\
`poverty_charities_count`, `debt_charities_count`, `housing_charities_count`, `domestic_abuse_charities_count` from Charity Commission\
`care_home_count`, `poor_cqc_count`, `mental_health_service_count`, `addiction_service_count`, `dentist_cqc_count` from CQC\
`gp_practice_count`, `pharmacy_count`, `dental_activity_proxy` from ODS/NHSBSA\
`pawnbroker_count` from chain locators + OSM/Overture/VOA\
`bank_branch_count`, `bank_closure_count`, `atm_count` from Geolytix/LINK\
`homeless_services_count`, `advice_services_count`, `law_centres_count` from Homeless Link / AdviceLocal / Law Centres\
`supermarket_count`, `discount_or_convenience_ratio`, `takeaway_to_supermarket_ratio` from Geolytix + FHRS\
`claimant_count`, `imd_score`, `fsm_share`, `overcrowding`, `no_car`, `private_rent_share` from Nomis/Census/IMD/DfE

For ward assignment: use **ONSPD/NSPL** for postcode-to-ward joins, ONS ward boundaries for coordinate spatial joins, and ONS LSOA-to-ward best-fit lookups for LSOA datasets. ONS postcode products and Open Geography give the lookup/boundary machinery you need. ([geoportal.statistics.gov.uk](https://geoportal.statistics.gov.uk/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Data.gov.uk](https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/8fd0e85c-3f46-4851-a041-e105b3b32315/lsoa-2021-to-electoral-ward-2025-to-lad-2025-best-fit-lookup-in-ew-v21?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/methodology/geography/geographicalproducts/postcodeproducts?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The main caution: don’t treat “more services” as automatically “more poverty”. Dense town centres attract services. Normalize against population, retail premises, high-street area, and urban/rural class. The more revealing measures are ratios: **pawnbrokers per bank**, **food banks per supermarket**, **betting premises per retail premises**, **debt/housing charities per total charities**, **poor-rated care per all care locations**, **takeaways per grocer**, and **support services versus deprivation need**.
